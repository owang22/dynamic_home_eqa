Memory that forgets on cue

What a home robot's memory must do when the household's routine shifts — a replay buffer, a nightly routine table and three counters on a scripted sick spell, scored on the questions where only a memory of the routine can answer.

The testbench

Ten simulated households (F1 regime, frozen 2026‑09‑21): 14 weekdays of an ordinary routine, ten days with one resident off sick — their book, tablet, mug, water bottle, charger, glasses, glass and razor migrate from the desk to the couch and coffee table — and eight days back at work. The robot sees one round at 03:00 plus the resident's "found it" answer ten minutes after every question; 12–16 questions a day about that resident's things, asked while they are in use. Every agent sees the identical stream.

Cold questions are the first about an object each day, before any feedback for it — a third of all questions. Warm questions are answered by recency at ~80% by everyone, last‑seen included; on cold questions last‑seen is at 14%, so cold accuracy measures the memory of the routine and nothing else.

How these contrasts are stated

Every comparison is the mean of paired per-household differences with its standard error and n; an effect is detected when it is at least twice its se, then judged small or large on its own merits. The household-to-household spread (sd) is reported beside it as heterogeneity, never as the test; below n=6 the stricter bar applies instead (the effect must exceed the spread) and we name the rule in force. We also say which claims pass that stricter bar, because several pass one and not the other — it was our original criterion and it is what caught a three-household claim of ours that did not replicate. The new bar is the more permissive of the two — for six or more households, exceeding the spread already implies exceeding two standard errors, because the spread is √n standard errors — so nothing stated under the old rule loses its standing and the change can only add claims.

Detected and above the spread: start message in the spell +23.5 (se 4.2) and +36.3 (se 5.9) cold; buffer 37.5 below the half-life counter in the spell (se 3.8) though level in the lead; buffer above the routine table in lead (+26.3, se 3.9) and return (+25.1, se 4.1); the half-life counter's return drop −22.9 (se 4.3) against the regime-tagged counter's −4.7 (se 1.3), all questions. Detected but below the spread: the un-retracted message's return-day cost, −7.0 all (se 2.2) and −12.3 cold (se 3.9) over 18 households — real, consistent in sign, but varying a lot per home. Not detected: the end message repairing the return (+8.9 cold, se 4.8; +4.8 all, se 2.9, 18 households) and the routine table's gain from the start message (+12.1, se 6.5).

The sample matters, visibly. Our first ten households were the optimistic half of both return numbers: the message's cost was −11.3 there and −1.6 on eight fresh households (pooled −7.0); the end message's repair was +13.4 there and +3.4 on the fresh eight (pooled +8.9). The spell effect, by contrast, replicates: +36.3 then +29.5, pooled +33.3 (se 3.7). Large and robust going in, real but smaller coming out.

While the message is true it is applied exactly where it belongs and the other resident's things do not move — the near-zero column with its small standard error is half the claim. Cold questions, spell cells: +21.8 (se 3.6) and +31.9 (se 3.5) on the sick person's things against +0.7 (se 2.4) on the other's. The leak is on the return: with the message still standing after the spell, the other resident's things are 3.9 points worse where the sick person's own are not measurably changed. Selective revision is a property of an instruction that still matches the evidence, not of the memory's indexing.

4 · Consolidation loses the routine

Three consolidated designs — a nightly rewrite from yesterday's sightings, a rewrite from the last seven days of per-object timelines, and free-form notes — plateau at 40–58% on cold questions in the lead and 19–22% in the spell. The mug's line in the seven-day table of household s0:

  • after day 1 mug_yuki: cupboard_k1 03:00-09:36; kitchen_table_k1 09:36-11:26; desk_b1 11:26-21:05; coffee_table_l1 21:05-23:59 (moved from desk_b1 to cupboard_k1 on day 1)
  • after day 3 mug_yuki: pantry_shelf_k1 03:00-09:38; desk_b1 09:38-23:59 (moved from sink_k1 to pantry_shelf_k1 on day 3)
  • after day 13 mug_yuki: nightstand_b1 03:00-15:05; desk_b1 15:05-15:48; sink_k1 15:48-16:47; desk_b1 16:47-22:17; coffee_table_l1 22:17-23:59 (routine changed day 13: starts on nightstand)
  • after day 16 mug_yuki: cupboard_k1 03:00-14:00; coffee_table_l1 14:00-23:59
  • after day 25 mug_yuki: cupboard_k1 03:00-09:27; kitchen_table_k1 09:27-12:52; desk_b1 12:52-23:59 (routine changed day 25: uses kitchen table then desk)

The breakfast (kitchen table) and evening-TV (coffee table) windows that the questions ask about are gone by day 3 although seven days of timelines are in the prompt. A "typical day" narrative keeps the modal story and drops the sparse in-use windows.

The LLM memories say ~0.87 whatever the stage; the counters are under-confident but their confidence moves, and the tagged counter's is lowest exactly when its bins are empty.

Is that constant a bug? No — checked, because a metric that does not move is more often broken plumbing than a real invariance. Per arm the LLM's confidence has sd 0.10–0.14 over 6–10 distinct values spanning 0.20–0.98, and it ranks answers usefully: accuracy above an arm's median confidence runs 21–42 points higher than below it. Only the stage mean is flat. So the model does express uncertainty about individual questions; what it never does is lower the whole distribution when the regime changes under it. Every arm also passes a degeneracy assay (answer concentration against the truth's own concentration, repeated-output share, confidence spread and discrimination): 2 parse failures in ~60,000 calls, no degenerate arm.

Controls that made this readable

  • Dated timestamps. With weekday-only stamps ("Fri 10:40") the model could not tell which of five Fridays a sighting was from and never learned the routine (v1, archived).
  • No weekday names. The F1 calendar runs the weekday routine every day; a prompt that named Saturdays had the model reasoning "she sleeps in" about a world that keeps no Saturdays (−20 to −25 on those days, v2, archived).
  • Cold / warm split, identical streams for every agent, a prompt leak check on every call, temperature 0 (not bit-reproducible across batch compositions on vLLM; each run's cache replays exactly).
  • Server: local vLLM, --max-num-seqs 64 --enable-prefix-caching, static prompt header first so the model's 784-token cache blocks hit.
