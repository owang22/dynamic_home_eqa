Dana and Felix at Home

Working towards a robot that finds a named thing in a home by searching well. This page holds the plan, the evidence behind it, a simulated household to explore, what has been measured so far, and every independent check that was run.

The aim

A robot that is asked for a specific thing in a home and finds it with few looks and little travel. It decides where to look next from what it has seen before and what it sees during the search, and it can move closer or to another side when it is not sure what it is looking at.

The work is in simulation first: a household whose belongings move about over a month as its residents follow daily routines, placed in a real 3D home. For ten days in the middle one resident is unwell and the routine changes. The real check is on a Unitree Go2 in one apartment and a lab space.

What would count as success

  • In simulation: on homes held back for testing, and with a detector in place of true labels, a policy finds things in fewer looks and fewer metres than last-seen and most-frequent, by at least twice the standard error across homes.
  • On the robot: what the Go2's camera shows from a set of positions in the real apartment agrees with what the simulator shows from the same positions in a scan of that apartment. This is counted over a few hundred looks, which is enough to measure; 20 searches are not enough to rank policies.
  • A result that would stop the work: the simulator's pictures do not predict what the robot sees, or no policy beats the mix of last-seen and most-frequent once a detector is in place.

These are proposed, for Oliver to change. No dates are set here.

What would be new

  • Belongings that move because residents follow routines, seen through rendered pictures where a look can miss the thing. The closest work uses true positions (FlowMaps), text (WorldLines), or moves things arbitrarily (EvoNav-Bench).
  • A look that found nothing is ambiguous: the thing moved, or it was there and not seen. The searches found nobody measuring this in a home with routines.
  • Search measured from a quadruped's camera height, where much of a home is out of view.
  • Last-seen and most-frequent rules on the same scale as learned predictors and language models, in looks and metres.

Decisions made by Oliver

  • Policies first. The starting policy is hand-built from where the thing was last seen and where it is most often. Learned policies come later and are judged against it.
  • Photos at chosen positions. The viewer has 42 positions in this home; more are needed. Continuous video is not needed yet.
  • Targets are named by appearance ("the squat navy mug with a round badge"). Naming by owner comes later.
  • No opening of cupboards or drawers. A Go2 cannot, so things inside them cannot be found.
  • The change of routine is one test condition, not the centre of the work.
  • Compute: one 96 GB GPU now, possibly one or two H100s.

What the evidence changed

  • Camera height limits what can be found. In this home, from 1.0 m everything is in view; from 0.30 m most of what is on tables and counters is not. How much is lost between those heights depends on how seeing is counted. See Findings.
  • Recognition is the weak part in real object search, more than exploring. Policies ranked with true labels can change rank once a detector is used, so every policy comparison is run both ways.
  • Simple explicit models do well on the number of looks; learned models do better on the first guess. Under a changed schedule one newer model beat a classical one by 11 points.
  • A reference picture did better than a description in the one comparison found, so the target is given both ways and the two are compared.
  • Do not rely on these renders to teach a model to see. The evidence is thin (10 real episodes) but points one way.
  • The real-robot results cited rest on 5 to 60 trials, most on 25 or fewer.

Each is traced to its source, with an independent checker's verdict, under Evidence.

Proposed goals, in order

1

Settle the camera, with the real robot in progress

  • Oliver's answers to the first two open questions below.
  • About ten photographs from the robot's own camera: a kitchen counter, a table, a bedside table and the floor, from near and from a few metres back.
  • The simulated study repeated at the robot's own camera height and lens, with the upward tilt the body allows.

Done when: a camera height and mount are chosen, and the simulator shows the same things in and out of view as the photographs do. Why first: every later number depends on it, and the photographs settle it faster than more rendering.

2

A search benchmark that runs on stored pictures next

  • A set of camera positions per home, with the walking distance between each pair.
  • Pictures stored for every position at every question time, so a search is a sequence of lookups and needs no rendering.
  • The robot ends a search by declaring a thing found, or by answering that it cannot be found. About a fifth of asked-about things are out of the house or in a closed cupboard at any moment.
  • The score: right thing found, wrong thing declared found, "cannot be found" when that is true or false, number of looks, metres walked. Reported by day, and split by whether the thing moved since it was last seen.

Done when: a policy can be run over a month of questions in seconds. Why: a look has no cost in the current setup, so searching well cannot be measured.

3

Measure perception on our pictures next

  • Three numbers, kept separate: does a detector find the thing; does matching against a reference picture pick the right one of two look-alikes; does a vision-language model choose correctly.
  • From these, the chance of seeing a thing that is there, by size, distance and contrast. Goal 4 needs this.
  • Two controls: two copies of the same model must come out at chance, and two different kinds of thing near 100%.

Done when: each number is reported with its spread over homes. Why before policies: one study in the same simulator lost about half its success when true labels were replaced, and these detectors have not been measured on picking out one specific object in our renders.

4

Reference policies, run twice next

  • Random; household common sense (where such a thing usually is); last-seen, going next to the places it was seen before that; most-frequent; a weighted mix of the two.
  • A rule that looks where (chance it is there × chance of seeing it if it is) ÷ walking distance is largest, and lowers a place's chance after a look that found nothing.
  • A per-place estimate of how long things stay where they were seen.
  • Two upper bounds: the best order given the true chances, and going straight to the true place.
  • Every policy is run once with true labels and once with the perception measured in goal 3.

Done when: looks and metres are reported for each, by day through the change of routine. Any learned policy has to beat these.

5

Moving closer or to another side later

Add near and far positions and other sides for each place. Decide when another view is worth its cost. 11 of our 24 objects can only be identified from some sides; the one study found says extra views reject more look-alikes and also more true matches.

6

More homes, and a check of the simulator itself later

  • Tens of homes with several households each, some held back for testing. First the hand-written link between places and furniture has to become automatic.
  • All of each household's things placed, not only the 13 asked about, so that rooms are as cluttered as they should be.
  • A comparison of where the generator puts things against a real record of where things are in homes.
7

The real check on the Go2 later

  • Scan the apartment with a phone and load the scan into the simulator.
  • From matched positions, compare what the robot's camera shows with what the simulator shows, over a few hundred looks.
  • Work out how the robot gets to a chosen position. The software for this exists but is reported to drift.
  • Then a small number of full searches, as a demonstration and not as a ranking of policies.
8

Learned parts, only where they beat the rules later

A learned predictor of where things will be; a small vision-language model tuned to choose among candidate positions. One published chooser of this kind was trained on four A100 GPUs.

Risks

  • The household is authored. How much sits on tables and counters is a setting of the generator, and hand-built rules are being tested on a hand-built generator.
  • One home so far, with dark walls and only 13 things placed.
  • 18 of the 24 object models have a recorded fault, and nothing renders see-through.
  • A raised camera changes the robot's balance and what it can walk under.
  • Getting the real robot to a chosen position is not solved here.
  • The literature was gathered in one day, preferring work since October 2024. Older work on searching for objects is thin here.

Open questions for Oliver

  1. Which Go2 is it (Air, Pro, X or EDU), and does it have an added camera or the expansion dock? Unitree supports programming on the X (partly) and the EDU; community software reaches the camera and movement on the others.
  2. Is a raised camera acceptable, and how high? In this home a camera at 1.0 m has everything in view; at 0.7 m what sits back on a counter is often missed.
  3. Can you take the ten photographs in goal 1?
  4. Is anything from this going to the workshop? Its deadline is 30 September 2026, 23:59 Anywhere on Earth.

Not now

  • Continuous video and world models.
  • Policies that go from pixels straight to motor commands. Most of what they learn is low-level movement, which chosen camera positions remove.
  • Animated residents.
  • Changing simulator. Habitat is no longer maintained by Meta beyond version 0.3.4.

Words used on this page

  • Moment: one day and time of the simulated month. 91 are rendered: 13 days at 7 times each.
  • Placement: one thing resting somewhere at one moment. 13 things over 91 moments give 1,183; in 917 the thing is somewhere it can be drawn, and in the rest it is in a closed cupboard or out of the house.
  • Look: one picture from one camera position.
  • In view: at least 200 of the thing's pixels are in a 960 by 720 picture, the edge of the picture does not cut it, and at least half of what it shows at its best is showing.
  • Clear: in view, at least 600 pixels, and not dark on a dark surface.

What others have found

Five literature searches were run on 30 September 2026, preferring work from October 2024 onward. The 22 claims this plan leans on hardest were then given to an independent checker with no knowledge of the plan, who read each paper in full.

"Partly" means the numbers were right but attached to the wrong condition, or a condition was left out that changes the reading. Those claims are reworded here to what the paper supports.

What a camera at each height can see

The same household at the same 91 moments, photographed from five camera heights. Each cell is the share of placements in which the thing is in view from that height. The Go2's own camera is at about 0.37 m.

Every place with something on it was photographed from up to eight camera positions in its room, chosen by the same rule at every height. "Best position" counts the thing if any picture taken at that moment shows it, which measures what the height allows. "Chosen position" uses only the first position for each place, which is what the viewer on this page does.

What holds under every way of counting that was tried: from 1.0 m everything is in view, and from 0.30 m most of what is on tables and counters is not. What does not hold steady: the sizes at 0.30 to 0.70 m. See the second table.

The 24 object models and their tags

Each model was rendered from four sides and tagged from those pictures. Two independent checkers then tested the tags: one described each picture without seeing the tags, the other matched the shuffled descriptions back to the pictures (24 of 24 correct).

Independent checks

Each check was done by a separate agent that was told as little as possible about how the work was made or what it was expected to show, and was asked to be blunt. Findings were compared with the evidence before acting on them; a few were misreadings and are listed as such.
